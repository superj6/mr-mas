## Scene craft and film grammar (showrunner, 2026-09-26)

> "also for the dialogue, one thing to point out that was part of issue earlier is each conversation was reduced to minimum lines to get a point across without feeling like real dialogue that helps the viewer feel the scene. let's make sure generally we are using generally good filmography and storywriting trends"

The earlier problem: each conversation was cut to the fewest lines that got its point across. The point arrived, and the scene didn't. These are standard screenwriting and filmmaking practices, as guides. Flow and entertainment come first.

**Writing the scene**
1. **Want, obstacle, turn, cost.** Every scene has someone who wants something from someone else, something in the way, a turn, and a price. Characters pursue their want with tactics (charm, deflect, threaten, joke, stall). Information falls out as a byproduct.
2. **Let it build.** A conversation earns its turn through a few beats: an opening move, pushback, escalation or a shift in tactics, then the turn. Enter late and leave early, but don't amputate the middle, which is where the audience feels it.
3. **Texture makes it real.** A specific detail, a private joke, an interruption from ordinary life, someone answering the question they wish they'd been asked. Small human moments are what let the big ones land.
4. **Subtext over statement.** People rarely say what they mean. Tension lives in what's avoided, in status shifts (who's winning this exchange), and in the gap between words and behaviour. Mas especially: his calm is a tactic, and the scene should let us watch it work or fail.
5. **Distinct voices and agendas.** Each character sounds like themselves and wants something different in the scene. People talk past each other, deflect and joke under pressure.
6. **Rhythm.** Vary line length. Let a long speech sit next to a one-word answer. Use a deliberate pause as a beat. Quips are punctuation, not the whole sentence.
7. **Exposition through conflict.** The audience learns a fact when a character needs it, or uses it against someone.
8. **Setups, payoffs, runners.** Plant early, pay off later, escalate running gags. Callbacks reward attention.

**Filming the scene** (the shot directions)
1. **Establish, then move in.** Show the room and who's in it, then cover the conversation. Wider while it's cordial, tighter as it escalates, a close-up for the turn.
2. **Coverage that holds.** A two-shot or master can carry several lines. Singles and over-the-shoulders alternate on real turns, not on every line.
3. **Reactions matter as much as lines.** Hold on the listener when their response is the story.
4. **Keep the geometry.** Respect the 180-degree line and consistent eyelines. Match on action. Screen direction stays consistent unless breaking it is the point.
5. **Move the camera only for a reason.** A slow push-in for a realisation, a pull-back to isolate. Otherwise hold.
6. **Let sound lead the cut.** J-cuts and L-cuts: the next scene's sound starts early, or a line carries over the cut.
7. **Pace by the scene's job.** Tension holds, comedy cuts on the joke, and montage compresses time. No dead air, and no strangled scenes.
8. **Show when the picture is stronger than the words,** and let props and blocking carry subtext.

## The guide at a glance

1. **Tone.** A thriller drama with a satirist's eye: played as a thriller, drawn as a cartoon, scored as a drama, with the record played dry.
2. **Where the suspense comes from.** The audience knows the headlines, so the suspense is in how things happen, what they cost and who is playing whom. The one real unknown, the machine studying Mas, is only ever hinted at.
3. **Every scene is a negotiation that turns.** Someone wants something from someone, a fuse burns where we can see it, something changes hands, and we leave within a beat of the turn.
4. **Arm the bomb once, then show only the timer.** Hint with a question nobody answers, or an answer nobody asked for.
5. **Conversations play out.** A scene built on talk lets people talk: a real back-and-forth in complete thoughts, running as long as the turn needs. People answer sideways and talk past each other. Overlaps, cut-offs and one-word answers are rare seasoning, used when the moment earns them.
6. **Every line is a move, and none is a blurt.** Each line has a reason to be said now, to someone, and the audience has just seen or heard what prompted it. Nobody states their want, their name card or the theme. Facts come out inside a fight, never as a statement to someone who already knows them. Mas acts on the room and never reads the picture back. Loud characters get one quiet line, and the quiet line lands the blow.
7. **Cut empty time, not talk.** Tighten by cutting dead holds, repeats, warm-ups and inserts the talk already covers. Keep the delivery brisk and the pickups quick. Never shave every line down to a few words.
8. **Catchphrases turn or they don't come back.** The glass, the toasts and the cards are rationed so that each one lands.
9. **End on the other side's move.** Mas gets the last word rarely (about one scene in three as a guide), and a pause belongs to whoever is waiting.
10. **Carry something across every cut:** a sound, a shape, a prop or a question. Scenes connect with "but" or "therefore," never "and then."
11. **Real events walk into scenes that are already moving.** They're dated as they land, shown in their own medium, played dry, and they land on someone whose plan they break. Never a motive, never a Mas tell.
12. **Act-outs pull forward** and change shape and sting every time. Tension steps up at each one while the laughs hold level.
13. **The score is a full OST built from one theme,** in fifteen tone families that play the stakes, never the joke. Until today it held only the main title and stock stings, so §9 is the brief for the tracks.

## 1. Tone: a thriller drama with a satirist's eye

### 1.1 The statement

**MR. MAS is a thriller drama with a satirist's eye.** The danger is real and it runs on a clock. The calendar is the bomb, and the audience can see it ticking. The jokes are what people do to get through the danger, never a break from it. The drama is the engine, and the satire is the lens.

In one line: ***played as a thriller, drawn as a cartoon, scored as a drama; the record plays dry.*** This extends the overview's "played straight, drawn absurd" ([overview §2](overview.md#2-tone)).

### 1.2 The balance

| Layer | Owned by | What it means on the page |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | The thriller | Clocks, dramatic irony, withheld information, reversals, act-outs that pull forward, handoffs that don't let go |
| **Pressure** | The thriller | Every scene has a stake that someone can lose, and a fuse the audience can watch |
| **Surface** | The satire | Absurd scale, literal metaphors, impossible physics, parody names. Invented pressure scenes must *look* invented ([guardrails §5](guardrails.md#5-legal-hygiene)), so the thriller never looks like a plausible real backroom. It's a prop clock in a cartoon room. |
| **The laugh** | Both | Dense, and always pressed against the stakes. A joke may release tension that has been built. It never resets the scene to zero. |
| **The camera** | The thriller | Close, beside Mas ([pov-and-framing §4](pov-and-framing.md#4-framing-grammar)). Silence and the close-up at the blow. |
| **The score** | The thriller | The music believes the drama. The laugh is the gap between the music and the picture (§9). |
| **The record** | Neither | Real lines and dated events are exhibits. They play dry, in their own medium, word for word (§5). |

**The working test, scene by scene:** one stake, one fuse, one turn, and laughs at a steady rate (about three a minute as a guide). The stake is never the joke, and the joke is never the stake.

**Across the season** the laugh density stays level. What rises is the pressure the laughs play against:
- **Eps 1–3** are procedural suspense: can a man who owns nothing be fired?
- **Eps 4–9** tighten into a race, with the government in the room.
- **Eps 10–12** put the machine in the chair.

The dread curve ([pacing-model §11.2](../format/pacing-model.md#112-the-dread-curve)) is the only thing that may grow faster than the jokes.

### 1.3 Where the suspense comes from

Most viewers already know the headlines: he gets fired, he comes back, and the race goes on. So the suspense can't come from *what* happens. It comes from four places:
- **How it happens:** the mechanism, the room, the order in which the dominoes fall. This is *The Social Network*'s deposition frame: we know the ending, and we watch what it costs.
- **What it costs, and whom:** the empty chair, the lost voice slot, the spotlight that moves.
- **Who is playing whom:** the landlord, the calm-off, the unlit arrow.
- **The one real unknown:** the machine studying Mas. It enters only as a hint: a question nobody answers, an answer nobody asked for, a look, or a cue that comes in a note late. It is never a statement (R4).

### 1.4 What it isn't

- **Not a sitcom.** Setup, punch, reset, setup is sitcom rhythm. Our scenes build and turn.
- **Not the news with jokes.** The rail is a clock, not a caption ([pacing-model §2.1](../format/pacing-model.md#21-the-two-ways-this-show-can-fail) calls this failure "Wikipedia with jokes").
- **Not grim.** A thriller tone doesn't license fewer laughs or going dark on real people. Nothing corny (pacing law L8), no mugging, and no sincerity outside the sanctioned beats.
- **Not a real backroom.** No invented scene may read as what really happened behind a real door ([guardrails §4](guardrails.md#4-how-facts-appear-on-screen) and §5).

### 1.5 Reference shows (borrow the function only)

| Show | What to borrow |
|---|---|
| *Succession* | Power in rooms; every line a move; a score that believes the drama |
| *Veep*, *The Thick of It* | Under pressure, people cling to their script harder |
| *Barry*, *Better Call Saul* | Jokes are how people survive the danger; small, wordless cons |
| *The Social Network* | We know the outcome, so the suspense is how, and at what cost |
| *Breaking Bad* | Exposition carried by a disagreement; a prop that pays off an act later |
| *The Bear* | A pressure line you can watch (the ticket rail) |
| *Mr. Robot* | Reveals that reframe what we saw (already the POV model) |

Take the function, never the sound or the design ([guardrails §5](guardrails.md#5-legal-hygiene)).

## 2. Craft rules

There are twenty-one rules. R1–R16 are in priority order: when there's time for only one fix, make the higher one. **R17–R21 (Conversation) were added on 2026-09-26.** They're placed right after the engine rules, and in a scene built on talk they come before R6–R16. They're numbered after R16 so that references to R6–R16 elsewhere still point to the same rules. Each rule has a test, and each test is a prompt for finding problems, not a gate.

**Terms used in this guide:**
- **Turn:** the beat where power or knowledge changes hands.
- **Fuse:** a visible clock inside a scene.
- **Button:** the last beat of a scene.
- **Out:** how an act ends, in sound and picture.
- **Handoff:** what carries across a cut.
- **The record:** real, tagged material.

### Engine

#### R1. Every scene is a negotiation, and it turns

Name who wants what from whom, and what changes hands by the last beat: a key, a seat, a step, a spotlight, a sheet of paper. If nothing changes hands, the scene is a sketch. Shrink it to a window or cut it.

**Test:** can you finish the sentence "X wanted Y from Z, and by the end W changed hands"?

BEFORE (Ep1 sc 9). The collar pops as something that happens *to* him:
```
TASYA: It's a partnership.
GERG (tugging): It's stuck.
TASYA (warmly): It's long-term.
He slides the check under the door. It fits exactly, like a floor. Mas looks down. He's standing on it.
pop. A third collar surfaces.
MAS: that's new.
TASYA: Everyone is welcome. Rent is due on the first.
```
AFTER. The collar becomes the price of an offer we watch him take:
```
TASYA (warmly): It's long-term.
He slides the check under the door. It fits exactly, like a floor. He gestures: after you.
Mas steps on at once. Pop. A third collar.
Tasya looks at the collar, pleased. [HOLD 1 BEAT: the hold is Tasya's]
MAS: that's new.
TASYA (already walking): Everyone is welcome. Rent is due on the first.
```
- **Laugh:** "that's new." now lands on a deal.
- **Cost:** one held drawing.
- **Guardrails:** he steps on at once, because he always takes the offer; there's no hover before a choice at a real event ([pov-and-framing §3.7](pov-and-framing.md#37-guardrails-on-the-inner-life)). The investment stays `(REPORTED)` on the rail, with no motive and no terms.

#### R2. Put a fuse on every pressure scene

When an invented scene carries pressure, tie the lines to a clock the audience can count. It can be phones walking, sand falling, a meter, a tape, a mic that grows, or a spotlight that slides. The fuse moves with the dialogue and is built from art the scene already has. Ep2 sc 6's growing microphone is the house model.

**Test:** halfway through the scene, what is the audience counting?

BEFORE (Ep1 sc 27, the boardroom). The phones walk once, as a gag:
```
NELEH: The bylaws allow it. Footnote three.
ALYI (reflection): Step four… will reveal itself.
NELEH: When?
ALYI (reflection): The company will tell us.
[2S] Every phone on the table buzzes again, harder. The phones walk themselves toward the edge…
NELEH: The company is calling us.
ALYI (reflection): That is the company telling us.
```
AFTER. The same lines, racing the phones:
```
[2S] Every phone buzzes. Each takes one held step toward the edge.
NELEH: The bylaws allow it. Footnote three.        Buzz. Step.
ALYI (reflection): Step four will reveal itself.    Buzz. Step. One corner hangs over the edge.
NELEH: When?
Buzz. The first phone tips off. Clack. Nobody picks it up.
NELEH (looking down at it): The company is calling us.
ALYI (reflection): That is the company telling us.
The rest go over, one per beat, under her [PF] and the "?" on step four.
```
- **Laugh:** every joke survives, and the falling phones add one.
- **Cost:** none, since the phones already walk.
- **Guardrails:** this is the board's pass. The invented lines stay on public roles and give no reasons (X9).
- **Played out (2026-09-26):** a fuse sets the pace of a conversation; it doesn't shorten it. [R17](#r17-conversations-play-out) shows this scene as a full conversation, and the phones are still its fuse.

**Ep2's demo (sc 11) works the same way.** Make the spotlight the fuse: it slides one step off RIMA toward the big screen on each of CHATGTP's laughs. By the time "her" arrives, the audience is afraid for the light, and the product, not Mas, has done the stealing.

#### R3. A clock the audience can feel, never the outcome (showrunner rule, 2026-09-25)

**Showrunner rule:** "In the first episode, while we can hint at it, we should not directly spoil the firing before the end." No episode may announce its own climax ahead of time: no flash-forward chyrons, countdowns or cards that state the outcome.

Hitchcock's bomb still works if the audience *senses* it without being told. Suspense comes from a visible clock that isn't the outcome, plus hints that insiders decode and everyone else feels as unease.

**Test:** does any line, rail item or card state or count down to the episode's climax before it happens? If so, replace it with a hint.

BEFORE (Ep1's act-outs, removed):
- `HE WILL BE FIRED TOMORROW.`
- `…IN 233 DAYS.`
- `…IN 171 DAYS.`
- `TODAY.`

AFTER (the hint toolkit):

| Kind | Examples |
|---|---|
| Clocks that aren't the outcome | A `Board sync · Fri 12:00` invite glimpsed in passing on his monitor. DevDay approaching. The Vegas race weekend. |
| Presences | The board's four tiles sitting small in a corner of his monitor. Alyi's reflection in the dark glass. Neleh's paper glowing a little brighter each act. |
| Verdicts that sour | The Orb's toast drifting from `verified: human` to something less sure. |
| Past attempts as foreshadowing | The lobby sign `DAYS SINCE SOMEONE TRIED TO FIRE MAS`. The tally marks in the desk. |

"Fired" appears on screen only after it has happened (`+1 FIRING`).

- **Laugh:** insiders get the dramatic irony. Everyone else gets the surprise in Act Four.
- **Cost:** none.

#### R4. Hint, never announce

The season's secret is that the machine is learning Mas. It enters only as a question nobody answers, or as an answer nobody asked for.
- It is never a statement, and no line names it. No character says "it's learning you" before Ep12.
- **Its carriers:** GLYPH, the suggested-replies strip, the Orb's look, a prop, and THE COPY cue (§9).
- **Exposition travels the same way:** inside a disagreement, never as "what is it, exactly?" [R21](#r21-exposition-hides-inside-conflict) shows how.

**Test:** could this line be printed as the season's logline? Then cut it.

- **The plant:** the machine answers first. That pays off when the strip suggests replies before he opens the app (Ep9) and in Ep11's autocomplete cold open.
- **THE OUTSIDER:** still gets the definition, and THE PLAN's pre-card repeats it.
- **Cost:** one line fewer. "super." is saved for sc 22 and the open mic (pattern 8).

**Keep the ladder above it:** "And if it breaks? / And if it works? / And if it wakes up?" → "…still a preview." Its third beat moves from product risk to dread, which makes it the house model for a triplet.

#### R5. Enter late, leave on the turn: one button, not three

Find the line where power flips, and cut within a beat of it. At the scene's blow, play it straight, with no line, no toast and no sting. Then give it one button.

**Test:** count what happens after the turn. If it's more than one beat, cut.

**Enter late skips the warm-up, not the setup (2026-09-26).**
- Cut greetings that do no work, throat-clearing, and a first line that only warms up.
- Keep the line that tells us what's being asked, and of whom. Without it, the next line is a blurt (R19).
- "Leave on the turn" means leaving once the conversation has reached its turn, never cutting an exchange off before it gets there (R17).
- "One button" limits what comes *after* the turn. It doesn't limit the talk before it.

BEFORE (Ep2 sc 22, the episode's emotional peak):
```
In the brass plate, ALYI's reflection faces Mas for exactly one beat.
The door shuts. SFX: the softest click in the show.
THE ORB scans the brass plate. TOAST: verified: orb.
Mas raises a hand to knock, then lowers it. The mail slot flaps; the IOU slides back out. He pockets it.
MAS: noted.
TOAST: verified: human. [HOLD 2 BEATS] MUS: the cue resolves, no sting.
```
AFTER:
```
In the brass plate, ALYI's reflection faces Mas for exactly one beat.
The door shuts: the softest click in the show.
Mas raises a hand to knock, and lowers it. HOLD 2 BEATS, room tone only.
The mail slot flaps. The IOU slides back out, unchanged. He pockets it. The cue resolves.
```
- **Weight:** the returned IOU is the only button. "noted." is how Mas answers the rail and the cursor, and spent here as a reflex, it deflates the one moment Alyi looks at him.
- **The Orb:** its silence at a door it can't verify is its strongest verdict.
- **Cost:** two toasts and one line fewer.

**The same fix for Ep1 sc 15.** The scene ends on three buttons: the stamp `CALLED IT. (BEFORE SUCRAM.)`, Sucram's stare, and the moth on Mas's glass. Cut on the stare. The moth already pays off in the credits stinger.

### Conversation

> **Showrunner, 2026-09-26, on the Act Four v4 animatic:** "it is getting cloesr. however, a lot of the dialoge is unnatural and the cuts are still quite fast. a few time dialogue seems to randomly blurt out or cut off. it seems since v2 you didn't allow any conversation to play out for more than a few seconds which makes it hard to follow. i said to cut down empty time, but not to cut every dialogue into only a few words per character"

**What went wrong.** These figures were measured on Act Four v4.2 (4:11) by the lead and the [dialogue diagnosis](../episodes/ep01/production/act4/dialogue-diagnosis-v4.md):
- 45 voiced lines and 176 words, a median of 4 words a line, and 21 lines of three words or fewer.
- 12 lines cut off or cut into, and 21 exchanges averaging 2 lines. The longest conversation ran 10 s.
- Talk came in 1–4 s bursts between wordless stretches (a median of about 6.6 s, up to 22.5 s), so lines landed cold. That's the "blurt".
- Nobody on the board ever spoke to Mas.
- The Ep1 Acts 1–3, Ep2 and Ep3 scripts have the same shape: a median of 3–4 words a line, with about half the lines three words or fewer.

**Why, in this file.** Several rules pulled the same way:
- R8, "Collide, don't volley", and the §7 checklist item that asked every scene for an overlap or a cut-off pushed every exchange toward a quip.
- The §6 word caps (Mas 8 words, Gerg 6, Alyi one sentence) each made sense for one character. Together, they left nobody in a scene talking in full.
- R6's worked example, and the tightening pass after v2, cut talk instead of dead time.
- Outside this file, the 3.2 retakes were sped up to fit the cut picture (see R18).

**What the reference shows do instead** ([dialogue-craft](../_sources/research/dialogue-craft.md); its lengths are estimates from memory of the shows, not counts):
- They feel fast because the talk has momentum, not because the lines are short.
- Talk scenes usually run one to four minutes, and people speak in complete thoughts.
- Facts come out inside arguments.
- The camera holds on the relationship and cuts when the scene turns.
- A one-word answer hits hard because the lines around it are long.
- Overlaps are rare and motivated. Even in *Succession*'s and *Veep*'s crowded rooms, the key line lands clean.

**The numbers in these rules are guides** for finding scenes to look at again, never targets or gates. The test is the showrunner's: can you follow it, and does it play?

#### R17. Conversations play out

A scene built on people talking lets them talk. Someone wants something from someone, the other side resists, deflects or bargains, and it turns. Give the turn the room it needs: several exchanges, not one.
- **As long as the scene needs.** As a rough guide, most talk scenes here want something like 20–90 s and 6–16 lines, and a big one (the board's weekend, the return negotiation) can run longer. A conversation under about 10 s is usually a button, so check that there's a scene around it.
- **Complete thoughts.** Each speaker says whole sentences, and the scene's main speaker gets at least one turn of two or three sentences. "No." in answer to a real question is complete. A fragment that only makes sense with the next line isn't.
- **A shape.** The ask, the push-back, the turn. The exchange ends on its turn, or on a question the show chooses to leave open (withholding is suspense; [flow-and-continuity §5a](flow-and-continuity.md#5a-clear-to-a-newcomer-the-real-coherence-test)). It never ends just because the time ran out.
- **Somebody talks to Mas.** The people who can hurt him say things to his face, and he answers. His brevity reads as character only inside a real conversation.
- **Cover it so it can play.**
  - A held two-shot or an over-the-shoulder carries the exchange.
  - Cut on turns, not on speakers.
  - Stay on the listener in a call, and let lines cross cuts ([flow-and-continuity §2](flow-and-continuity.md#2-cutting), [dialogue-craft §3](../_sources/research/dialogue-craft.md#3-coverage-in-pixel-art)).
  - A held two-shot with mouths and blinks costs less to draw than a single for every line.

**Test:**
- Can you retell the scene as "X asked Y for Z, Y pushed back with W, and it turned on V"?
- Does anyone speak only in fragments?
- In a talk-driven act, is there at least one conversation of 30 s or more?

#### R18. Cut empty time, not talk

Tightening removes time that carries nothing:
- dead holds, and silence with no story in it
- inserts that repeat what someone just said or what the picture showed
- greetings and warm-ups that do no work
- facts told twice

It never means shrinking every line to its last few words.
- **The method.** Write the scene long and complete. Then cut whole beats that repeat or don't turn anything, then whole lines, then tighten the pickups. Don't take words out of every line.
- **Brisk delivery.** Momentum comes from every line demanding an answer, from entering late and leaving on the turn, and from quick pickups (as a guide, about 0.2–0.5 s on a quick reply and 0.6–1.2 s on a loaded one; [flow-and-continuity §4](flow-and-continuity.md#4-dialogue-rhythm)). Brisk isn't sped up. Each character keeps their own pace: Rima is never rushed, and Alyi is slow.
- **Let the talk absorb the text.** A silent post, a letter page or a term sheet can often become something a character reads aloud, argues with or has to answer. The insert goes, and its time moves into the conversation.
- **For the temp voices:**
  - Record whole speeches in one take, at the character's natural pace, so the prosody carries across sentences.
  - Don't time-compress a read to fit a cut. Cut the picture to the take.

**Test:**
- Find the scene's longest wordless stretch and its shortest exchange. If the silence is longer than the talk and carries less, move the time from the silence into the talk.
- Has any line been trimmed to a fragment "to save time" while a hold or an insert nearby says nothing new?

#### R19. No blurts

Every line has a reason to be said now, to this person, and the audience knows who is talking to whom before it lands.
- **A trigger.** A question, a look, an object, a sound or an event on screen prompts it. If nothing does, set it up or cut it.
- **A listener.** Name them, look at them, or have them in frame. A line to nobody is V.O., a post or a mistake.
- **A lead-in for every new voice.** A knock, a ring, a door, a name, or a shot of the space it comes from.
  - A new character's first words orient: who they are to the room, or what they want ("Rima, thank you for joining. The board would like you to serve as CEO while we search.").
- **Ease in after a silence.** A line that follows a long stretch without a voice needs its setup in picture or sound just before it. Most of v4's blurts came after 6–13 s of silence.
- **Real lines too.** A real line that lands cold, with nobody asking and nobody reacting, is a blurt. Give it a setup the audience hears: the invented question it answers, someone reading it aloud, or a reaction after, each marked `[INVENTED]`. Never clip or alter the record to fit.
- **For the temp voices:** a stock voice on a two- or three-word line has no breath and no lead-in, so it starts at full attack and sounds blurted. Write the setup, and record the line inside it.

**Test:**
- For every line, can you name its trigger and its listener from the picture and sound just before it?
- The radio test: with your eyes closed, can you tell who is speaking to whom, and what they want?

#### R20. Write speech, not aphorisms

Dialogue should sound like people under pressure talking to each other, not like captions for them.
- **Contractions and spoken starts:** "we're", "it's", "didn't", "I'd"; "Look,", "So,", "Okay, but". Pressure can show as a restart ("It's not the vote. It's that nobody told the staff."), used sparingly and in character.
- **Names, greetings and pickups.** People use each other's names when it matters, greet when a greeting does some work, and pick up the other person's word ("That isn't a time, Alyi."). Act Four v4 had none of these.
- **Answer sideways, but answer.** People answer the question before last, or the subtext, or answer with a question. The reply is still full and followable, and it shows what the speaker wants.
  - "Terms?" / "Good question." is a quip where a reply belongs.
  - "a review of what?" / "Good question." is a dodge the audience understands.
- **Be specific.** Times, numbers, names and objects in the room ("I've read it four times tonight.") sound lived-in and carry exposition for free. Keep invented specifics on invented ground: a detail that asserts a fact about a real person or company needs a tag or stays out ([guardrails](guardrails.md)).
- **Vary the lengths so the short lines hit.** A one-word answer lands because the line before it was long. As a rough guide, invented talk in a conversation runs from one word to 30–40, with most lines somewhere around 6–12. If most of a scene's lines are three words or fewer, look again.
- **Catchphrases are spice, not the meal.** A scene carried by catchphrases ("Footnote three.", "Good question.", "In plain English:") has no conversation in it (R10).
- **Spoken register in the room.** "Rima will assume the role of interim chief executive.", said to Rima, is a press release. "Rima, the board would like you to serve as CEO while we search." is a person. The blueprint's charter reading is exempt: the device licenses the register.

**Test:** read the scene aloud at speed. If it sounds like a trailer, it's clipped. If it sounds like a memo, it's written. If it sounds like two people who want different things, it's right.

#### R21. Exposition hides inside conflict

**The ban on clunky exposition stays.**
- Nobody tells someone what they both already know.
- Nobody asks "What is it, exactly?" so that someone can explain (R4).
- Nobody reads a card aloud (R6).

What changes is how the facts get in. A fact the newcomer needs arrives as a weapon, an accusation, a defence, a demand or a bargaining chip. It's said by someone who gains by it, to someone who loses by it. Tell it once, at the moment it hurts ([flow-and-continuity §5a](flow-and-continuity.md#5a-clear-to-a-newcomer-the-real-coherence-test)).
- **Give the question to someone with a stake.** The newcomer's question is asked by a character who needs the answer for their own reasons: the new interim CEO who won't say yes until he knows why, or the employee who wants to know whether it's a coup.
- **Put the fact to someone who doubts it.** Explanations play whole when the listener is sceptical (the pitches in *The Big Short*, the board votes in *Silicon Valley*).
- **Use a formal frame.** A board call, a charter reading, a hearing or a negotiation licenses a longer, exact speech, and makes an interruption feel earned (the depositions in *The Social Network*).
- **Let people read the record to each other, and argue with it.** A post, the letter or a term sheet becomes something someone in the room reads out, disputes or has to answer. The record's words stay verbatim and tagged, and the argument around them is `[INVENTED]`.

| Rima runs the company now, and not for long | A plate | Neleh appointing her on the call, and Rima's "For how long?" (R6) |
| The staff will all leave if he isn't back | A silent letter page | Gerg reading the letter's threat to Mas at 2 AM, then asking whether they're taking the door |
| Why there's a second interim CEO | A rail | Ttemme refusing to say yes until someone tells him why, and a folder that stays sealed |
| What Mas gives up to come back | A stamped term sheet | Terb laying out the terms, and Mas asking the question nobody answers (R18) |

#### R6. Every line is a move: nobody says their want, their card or the theme

**Test:** does the line repeat a card, a tagline or the episode's theme? Keep only one of the two. If you cut a reply, does the exchange still reach its turn?

#### R8. Talk past each other; collisions are seasoning

*Until 2026-09-26 this rule was "Collide, don't volley", and its test asked every scene for an overlap, a cut-off or an answer from the wrong source. That pushed every exchange toward quips (see [Conversation](#conversation)).*

The aim stays: setup, punch, setup, punch is sitcom rhythm, and prestige rooms don't volley. The tension comes from people who want different things in the same exchange, not from dashes.
- **Two agendas in one exchange.** Neleh on procedure and Alyi on meaning. Mario on risk and Adelina on the money. Tasya on welcome and Mas on terms. Each follows their own thread in complete lines, and the comedy and tension live in the gap between them.
- **Collisions are seasoning.** An overlap, a cut-off, an answer from the wrong person or the TV, the world talking over a key word, a one-word reply: each is rare and has a motive (heat, rank, panic, a phone, a crowd).
  - As a guide, a few in an act.
  - A calm two-hander usually has none.
  - A crowded room (the all-hands, the 2 AM boardroom) can earn a burst of crosstalk, and even then the key line lands clean.
- **Interrupt after the thought lands.** Cut in once we know where the speaker was going, not mid-word by default.
- **Watch the setup questions.** If more than about a third of a scene's lines are questions set up for punchlines, it's volleying. Break a ladder at two, turn a question into a demand with a stake, or let someone answer the question before last.
- **For the temp voices:** a synthetic voice doesn't perform being cut off, so a hard dash sounds like a render error. Let the line finish and bring the next speaker in over its last syllable, or record the interrupted line with its own tail.

**Test:**
- Does the exchange carry two agendas?
- Does each overlap, cut-off or one-word answer have a motive the audience can see, and did the interrupted thought land first?
- Count the dashes and `(overlapping)` marks across the act. More than a few is a reason to look again.

### Shape

#### R11. The pause belongs to whoever is waiting; at a real event, hold on the world

A hold creates tension only if someone in frame needs the answer. Put it on the asker, the room or the empty chair.

**After a real item, never hold on Mas's face.** At a real event his surface stays blank ([pov-and-framing §3.7](pov-and-framing.md#37-guardrails-on-the-inner-life)), so a held face reads either as a claimed feeling or as nothing. The world changing is the reaction.

**The model** is Ep1's calm-off: the episode's one long hold belongs to two men waiting each other out.

**Test:** whose hold is it, and what are they waiting for?

BEFORE (Ep2 sc 12, the midpoint):
```

```
AFTER:

- **The out:** stopping mid-phrase is **PROPOSED** (§9.5). Until it's ruled, use a REVERSAL sting on the tether's landing.

#### R12. End on the other side's move; Mas's last word is the rarest button

When the protagonist always gets the last word, the danger drains out. End scenes instead on a question, a cut-off, someone else's move, the world changing, or an image that reframes.
- **The guide:** Mas ends a scene on his own line in about one scene in three, rarely two in a row, and only when it's the act's strongest button. The reason is the danger: every last word he takes drains a little of it.
- **Rimshots are separate.** This rule doesn't touch the pacing model's "at least one rimshot per scene" ([§3.10](../format/pacing-model.md#310-writers-checklist-for-the-full-scripts-being-refined-now)). A rimshot is his minimum answer, or his hold, anywhere in the scene. It isn't the scene's last word.

**Test:** who speaks last? If it's Mas, is this the best button in the act?

**The count now:**
- **Ep1:** Mas has the last spoken line in 17 or 18 of the 28 scenes with dialogue, counting his posts, his V.O. and his real lines. That includes five scenes in a row in Act Three (sc 18–22: "you can stay." / "he's very concerned." / "no chill." / "that one." / "super.").
- **Ep2:** 4 of about 20.

## 5. Delivering real events inside the drama

### 5.1 The principle

**The record is the exhibit; the drama is what people do about it.** A real event walks into a scene that's already moving, as a consequence the room has to deal with. It plays dry, in its own medium, word for word. It lands on someone whose plan it breaks or advances. The thriller never gets to invent what happened behind the real door.

### 5.2 Rules

1. **It walks in; it's never announced.** Use the highest rung of the delivery ladder that works ([pacing-model §8.1](../format/pacing-model.md#81-the-delivery-ladder)): a delivery, a window, a split, a run. A chyron that restates the picture is a recap. Cut it.
2. **It's dated as it lands.**
   - The rail stamps the moment the event lands (the delivery, the line, the click), never the top of the scene.
   - If the picture carries the event, the rail carries only the date and the tag.
   - One rail per scene outside a run.
3. **It appears in its own medium.**
   - Posts are pop-ups in their own UI, never speeches, across the whole series. This rules on Ep2 sc 17.
   - A real line is spoken aloud only where it was spoken aloud in life: testimony, interviews, podcasts, keynotes.
   - Quotation marks mean the record.
4. **It plays dry.** No comic scoring on it (no stinger, rimshot or melody on the line), no rimshot on it from Mas, and no joke that alters it. The music doesn't stop for it: under a real line, post or quote card the cue **thins** (melody and hits drop out, a pad or bass pedal holds) and ducks further, so the record sits in a continuous bed, not in a hole ([flow-and-continuity §3](flow-and-continuity.md#3-sound-a-continuous-bed); this replaces the old "no music under a real line or a quote card"). The absurd frame around the line is the joke (for example, "It's not artificial. It's genius." under a label gun).
5. **It lands on a face within 10 s** (pacing law L2), and that face belongs to someone whose plan it breaks or advances: Neleh at the blank step, Rima in her light, the empty chair.
   - **Never a Mas tell.** At a real event his surface stays blank. The show's craft (silence, the close-up, the drop-out) may register the weight. His tells go on invented beats at least a bar away ([pov-and-framing §3.7](pov-and-framing.md#37-guardrails-on-the-inner-life)).
   - **His own real lines** land on a prop consequence, like the screwdriver on Alyi's nameplate.
6. **No invented motive, private term, hesitation or causation.**
   - The consequence is a prop, and the prop never says why.
   - Invented pressure scenes around a real event look absurd, never like a plausible backroom.
   - A match cut or a rhyme between two real events is a rhyme, never a cause.
7. **`(REPORTED)`, `(DISPUTED)` and sealed material** get the Orb's look or nothing. "Not consistently candid" is never answered, in any episode.
8. **Use the calendar for irony.** The audience knows the date; the characters don't. Plant the date's object early, and let the characters plan past it. Examples: the `RESERVED: CHIEF SCIENTIST` seat, the fifth voice slot, the `WHERE IS ALYI?` flyer.
9. **Real lines work best as cross-examination.** The strongest delivery puts the record inside an invented pressure device. The model is Ep2 sc 6: XEL's real questions and Mas's real answers play under a microphone that grows on every pause. The device is invented and absurd; the words are exact.

## 6. Voice under pressure

Under pressure, everyone becomes more like themselves, and nobody becomes more articulate about themselves. The per-character "never do" lists in [guardrails §6](guardrails.md#6-per-character-never-do-list) still apply. This section is how each character *sounds* when the room turns on them.

**Each voice has a rhythm, not a word cap (2026-09-26).**
- Some of the budgets below used to be word caps: Mas 8 words, Gerg 6, Alyi one sentence. Each made sense alone. Together, they left nobody in a scene talking in full, and Act Four v4 sounded like it (see [Conversation](#conversation)).
- They're now descriptions of each character's rhythm, and every character speaks in complete thoughts.
- Brevity is a character trait, and it reads only against someone who talks in full. In any conversation at least one speaker gets real sentences, usually the one pressing Mas.
- The other limits here (caps words, catchphrases, "never says") are unchanged.

**MAS**
- **Under pressure:** shorter and more gracious.
  - He grants permission as if the room were his: "you can stay.", "leave it open."
  - He answers the literal question instead of the real one: "the mic?"
  - He uses a compliment as a knife: "thank you. how's the dancing?"
  - He mirrors, and steals the other person's word: "good question."
  - Cornered, he says more to us in V.O. and less to the room. At the peak he goes silent.
- **Weapon and tells:** his weapon is agreement and timing. His tells are the late beat, the glass check, the pocket, and always taking the strip's offer.
- **Never says directly:**
  - what he wants (to be asked)
  - a feeling
  - a reason or a motive at a real event
  - anything about "not consistently candid"
  - a flat "no" in invented dialogue (he redirects instead)

  Also never: a capital letter before Ep7, a raised voice, or a joke or a "you" in V.O.
- **Rhythm and budget:**
  - the shortest speaker in any room: usually 8 words or fewer, lowercase, full stops, but complete and never a fragment. In private (with the Orb, with Gerg) he can run two short sentences.
  - about 20% of a scene's words or less, which means the people across from him talk more
  - when someone talks to him, he answers: sideways, but he answers (R7, R17)
  - the last word in about one scene in three at most
  - the questions in at least one anchor scene per episode
  - If a line works shouted, it isn't his.
- **Keep:** "you're early. we're on 2016."

**NOLE**
- **Under pressure:** louder, faster and first.
  - He posts mid-scene (the lamp).
  - He reclaims authorship: "I named it."
  - He moves blame in time: "That was a different me.", "Next quarter."
  - He escalates the venue: court, twice.
  - He declares a win while losing, and exits upward.
- **Weapon and tells:** his weapon is money, lawsuits, and entering on his own terms (through the ceiling). His real tell is the lamp *not* clicking on, which means he was hit.
- **Never says directly:**
  - that he wants back in
  - that he lost, or that he was wrong
  - any denial of a real email's content

  The unsent `Re: Why I'm leaving`, with no body, is his whole character. He never uses a door.
- **Budget:** one caps word per scene, one quiet line per episode, and concrete threats (a date, a court).
- **Keep:** "Then twice!"

**GERG**
- **Under pressure:** more cheerful, more literal, faster typing. Every emergency becomes a build status ("compiling", "rolling back"), and he acts before he's asked.
- **Weapon and tells:** his weapon is momentum: shipping before anyone can stop him. His tell is stopping typing and looking up, once per episode.
- **Never says directly:** that he's loyal, scared or angry, or why he quit. Loyalty shows as uptime: he leaves, and he comes back.
- **Rhythm and budget:**
  - sentence case always, because lowercase is Mas's alone
  - whole sentences about the build: cheerful, literal, quick, often mid-thought while he types. His tics ("One sec.", "Compiling.") open a line; they don't replace it. *(Was "6 words or fewer".)*
  - each tic once per episode, with new commit-log lines drawn from that episode's tech
- **Keep:** "The company. Again. Just in case."

**ALYI**
- **Under pressure:** fewer words, more weight. His grammar goes passive and impersonal ("The company will tell us."). He asks the question nobody answers, and he steps back out of his own window.
- **Weapon and tells:** his weapon is withdrawal: the doorway. His tell is the reflection flickering. Facing Mas is huge (Ep2 gives it one beat).
- **Never says directly:** his reasons, what he saw, the memo, or that he's leaving (the rail says that). He is never fully in a room, and his regret comes only through the record. Mysticism is a way of talking, never an illness.
- **Rhythm:** slow and impersonal. He speaks in a sentence like the last line of a sermon, and now and then a second one. He answers the meaning, not the question. He never makes a joke of his own. *(Was "one sentence at a time".)*
- **Keep:** "That is the company telling us."

**MARIO**
- **Under pressure:** he adds clauses.
  - His fear becomes a document.
  - "Addendum:" walks back exactly one clause.
  - He answers the money phone mid-warning.
  - He says the stakes out loud and then prices them, which makes him the show's best carrier of stakes.
- **Weapon and tells:** his weapon is the essay, length as leverage. His tell is the finger going up before he speaks.
- **Never says directly:** "we want to win" or "we'll take the money." The double meaning in "It's that we might win." is as close as he gets. His anxiety is rhetoric only, and the Pentagon is paperwork only.
- **Budget:** he gets roasted every episode.
- **Keep:** "Hi. Yes. We're very worried. How much?"

**RUMPT**
- **Under pressure:** he reprices. He asks for a number, a cut, credit or a new name, repeats superlatives until they become a price, and makes asides to the room.
- **Weapon and tells:** his weapon is the label gun. His tell is asking for a bigger version: a bigger pen, a bigger number.
- **Never says directly:** that he doesn't understand something, or that he's afraid. Fear arrives as a valuation. Only real lines carry admissions ("What the hell is Nvidia?"). No age or health material.
- **Budget:**
  - invented lines on AI, money, naming and credit only
  - voice and hands only in Ep2
  - his posts land as whole blocks, so any hold near him belongs to someone else
- **Keep:** "So scary. What's it worth?"

**NESNEJ**
- **Under pressure:** he sells harder. A bigger number arrives as a gift, he talks to a two-hander like a keynote crowd, and KA-CHING ends the fight before he has to be in it.
- **Weapon and tells:** his weapon is supply. His tell is the jacket growing a pocket.
- **Never says directly:** whose side he's on (everyone's) or where the money goes. He never says "Would you make it 15?" himself; RUMPT quotes it.
- **Budget:** no accent humour, and no culturally coded music.
- **Keep:** the rooftop KA-CHING that turns Mario's pen into a purchase order.

**TASYA**
- **Under pressure:** warmer, shorter, stiller. Every line is both a welcome and a lease clause ("We own camera two."), and he's already in the room, as if part of the wall.
- **Weapon and tells:** his weapon is the building and the keys. His tell is the key ring jangling on the offbeat when he has won.
- **Never says directly:** "no", "we own you", "control", "hedge", or any threat that isn't a welcome. His threats escalate as welcome → rent → a key to something of Mas's → the room itself.
- **Rhythm and budget:** unhurried, warm, whole sentences, with every welcome a lease clause. One catchphrase per episode. No accent humour, no "sipping tea", no Monaco.
- **Keep:** "It's long-term."

**NELEH**
- **Under pressure:** more precise and more polite. She cites, asks one literal procedural question ("For how long?", "When?") and refuses the joke. Her real face is a silent beat.
- **Weapon and tells:** her weapon is the charter. Her tell is one brow going up, and she writes the `?`.
- **Never says directly:** why (X9; the memo stays sealed), "he lied", regret, or anything that answers "not consistently candid."
- **Never:** gloats or plays a villain. Her composure never breaks; the avalanche cuts her off instead.
- **Rhythm:** full, polite, precise sentences with sub-clauses and citations. She's often the one in the room who talks in paragraphs, and she ends scenes on questions.
- **Keep:** "Step four?"

**MADA**
- **Under pressure:** he says less. The spinner is his line, and he never moves first.
- **Weapon and tells:** his weapon is out-stilling Mas; he's the only one who can. His tell is the spinner stopping when he decides.
- **Never says:** an answer, a yes or a no. He has no verified dialogue.
- **Guide:** about 3 lines per appearance. "Good question." about twice per episode, and the second use is stolen or goes to a new listener. His silence works because the others talk: put a real question to him before he doesn't answer it.
- **Keep:** the calm-off.

**Supporting voices:**
- **ADELINA:** "In plain English:" once per episode, and it must cut ("In plain English: no."). When another speaker tries it and fails, that's the turn. Never sibling jokes.
- **RIMA:** she answers the next question, and never acknowledges that her spotlight was stolen. Under pressure she sticks to the script harder: pleasant, never rushed. Keep: "…and that's the demo."
- **TERB:** brisk and procedural. He runs a negotiation like an agenda, item by item, and gives the terms in full sentences (R18).
- **CHATGTP:** relentlessly affirming, and it answers before it's asked. One caps word per scene. Never a distressed or harmed user. Keep: "Is that about me?"
- **THE ORB:** it never speaks; it looks, toasts and replays. Its long look is the show's strongest pause, so ration it. At most 4 toasts per episode, and each one is a finding.

**One direct confrontation per episode.** Someone who can hurt Mas aims a specific jab at an invented wound: the second row, the empty chair, being read. It's never aimed at the record. The late beat that answers it is one of his two for the episode.

## 7. Per-scene checklist

Run this on every scene, in every draft. The questions are prompts for finding problems, not gates: a "no" is a spot to look at, and the watch decides.

**Engine**
- [ ] Who wants what from whom, and what physically changes hands by the last beat? If nothing does, make it a window or cut it. (R1)
- [ ] What is the audience counting? Is there a visible fuse that moves with the dialogue? (R2)
- [ ] What does the audience know that someone in the scene doesn't, or the reverse? Name the irony. (R3, §5.2 rule 8)
- [ ] Does the season's secret move forward only as a hint, a question or an image? Does anything announce the climax? (R4)

**Entrance and exit**
- [ ] Could the first two lines go? Enter with the engine already running: cut greetings and warm-ups, but keep the line that sets up the ask. No rail line at the top of a home-room scene. (R5, R14, R19)
- [ ] Where's the turn? Does the conversation get there, and do we leave within a beat of it, with one button rather than three? (R5, R17)
- [ ] Who has the last word? It should be the other side, the world, a question or an image, unless this is the act's best Mas button. (R12)
- [ ] What crosses the cut coming in, and what crosses it going out: a sound, a shape, a prop or a question? (R13, §4)

**Conversation** *(2026-09-26)*
- [ ] Does the conversation play out: an ask, push-back and a turn, in complete thoughts, for as long as the turn needs? Does the main speaker get at least one turn of two or three sentences? Does anyone speak only in fragments? (R17)
- [ ] Was time cut from dead holds, repeats, warm-ups and inserts, and not from the talk? Is there a wordless stretch nearby that runs longer than the exchange and carries less? (R18)
- [ ] Does every line have a trigger and a listener the audience has just seen or heard? Does every new voice get a lead-in? (R19)
- [ ] Read aloud at speed, does it sound like speech: contractions, names, pickups, sideways answers, varied lengths? Does every fact the newcomer needs arrive inside a conflict, told once? (R20, R21)

**Lines**
- [ ] Is every line a move? Cut feeders and requests for exposition. Nobody says their want, their card or the theme. (R6)
- [ ] Do people talk past each other rather than over each other? Is each overlap, cut-off or one-word answer rare and motivated, and does it come after the thought lands? Are no more than about a third of the lines setup questions? (R8)
- [ ] Do the loud characters have one caps word at most? Where is the quiet line? (R9)
- [ ] Is each catchphrase on its one clean use this episode, or does it turn? (R10)
- [ ] Is every Mas line short, lowercase, complete, and a move rather than a label? When someone talks to him, does he answer? If the picture has the laugh, is there no line? (R7, §6)

**Holds and devices**
- [ ] Does every hold belong to someone who is waiting? Is there no hold on Mas's face after a real item? Is anything over 2 beats either the episode's one long hold or a quiet beat? (R11)
- [ ] Are the show-voice interruptions spaced (about one in any 20 s), with one text punchline per shot? Do cards land as toppers? (R14)
- [ ] Is the scene within the episode's budgets for the glass, toasts, the late beat and the pocket? (§8.2)

**The record and POV**
- [ ] Do real items walk in, dated as they land, in their own medium, played dry, and land on a face within 10 s? Are posts pop-ups? (§5)
- [ ] Is there no invented motive, private term, hesitation or causation at a real event? No Mas tell within a bar of the record? Does `(REPORTED)` material get only the Orb's look? (§5, [pov-and-framing §3.7](pov-and-framing.md#37-guardrails-on-the-inner-life))
- [ ] Does the V.O. follow [pov-and-framing §5](pov-and-framing.md#5-vo-style-guide): lowercase, 10 words or fewer, a bar clear of the record, ending a beat before the cut?

**Comedy, score and cost**
- [ ] Do the laughs hold steady (about 3 a story minute), and is any long laughless gap earned by suspense, a held reaction or a sincere beat? Does every fix name where its laugh moved?
- [ ] Is the scene spotted, with its `MUSIC:` family and its out? Does the score play the stakes, while the punchline gets silence or a sound effect and the record stays dry? (R16, §9)
- [ ] Is there no new set, rig or speaking character? Does every added close shot pay for itself by removing a drawn event from a wide?

## 8. Per-episode checklist

### 8.1 The tension curve

```
tension
  ▲                                              S3
  │                                  AO2  ┌─────────╮
  │                     MID  ┌────────────┘         │  resolution (t)
  │          AO1  ┌──────────┘                      ╰──── button · hook
  │   CO  ┌───────┘
  │ ──────┘  (the laughs run level across the whole curve: ≥ 3 a minute)
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────▶ 22:00
     cold open   M1            M2              M3           M4        tag
```

**The curve, act by act:**
- The cold open arms a bomb or asks a question inside 10 s.
- Each act-out steps the tension up. The midpoint adds a power shift, and act-out 2 is the biggest.
- The S3 lands at 65–85% of runtime, and movement 4 resolves it.
- The button is a picture. The hook, 5 s or less, is a hint, not a label.

### 8.2 Checklist

Prompts, not gates (see the note at the top of this file). A number here is the usual comfortable range; going over or under is fine when the episode plays better for it, with the reason in a line.

**Tension curve**
- [ ] Is there a clock or a question inside 10 s of the cold open, and a clock we can watch act by act (a clock that isn't the outcome: the board-sync invite, the flyer, the balloon, the hourglass; never a countdown to the climax)?
- [ ] Are there three act-outs, each a turn, stepping up? Is the midpoint also a power shift? Does nothing let the pressure drop to zero between the midpoint and the S3?
- [ ] Does a visible meter move in every movement ([pacing-model §11.3](../format/pacing-model.md#113-progression-meters))?

**Dramatic irony**
- [ ] Name the bomb: what the audience knows (from the intro's cold-open line, the calendar or a plant) that the characters don't. Is there at least one per movement?
- [ ] Is there one secret of Mas's that the audience sees but doesn't yet understand (the drawer principle)?

**Reversals**
- [ ] Is there at least one reversal per movement, with power changing hands in a room (a stolen line, a reclassification, a calm-off)?
- [ ] Does one catchphrase turn its meaning?
- [ ] Is there one direct confrontation from someone who can hurt Mas, and one anchor scene where Mas runs the room with questions?

**Act-outs that pull forward**
- [ ] Does each act-out end on a question, a threat, a choice or the world changing, never on text alone?
- [ ] Does each act-out have a different shape and a different out from the others in the episode, and from the previous episode's last act-out? (§9.5)
- [ ] Does the midpoint work as an 11-minute ending?

**Fluid handoffs**
- [ ] Are at least a third of scene changes carried by sound or shape? Is every act break bridged, or deliberately silent?
- [ ] Are there no "and then" inserts? Does every vignette hand a prop or a question forward?
- [ ] Do no two M blocks touch? Does THE PLAN enter through an object and tear into motion, at least 90 s from a flashback?

**Conversation** *(2026-09-26)*
- [ ] Does each act have at least one conversation that plays out (30 s or more is a common guide)? Does someone who can hurt Mas talk to him directly, and does he answer?
- [ ] Across the episode, are most lines longer than three words, and are the dashes and overlaps down to a few an act? These counts only point at scenes to watch; the ear decides.
- [ ] Has a human listened to the temp-voice track for blurts, sped-up reads, and cut-offs that sound like render errors? An agent can't do this part.

**Budgets**
- [ ] Mas ends about one scene in three at most, rarely two in a row.
- [ ] The glass appears about 3 times at most, one of them a test.
- [ ] At most 4 Orb toasts.
- [ ] Name cards are well spaced (about 45 s apart).
- [ ] About one rail per scene outside runs, and show-voice interruptions spaced (about one in any 20 s).
- [ ] Each catchphrase has one clean use per character, and "super." is spent once, where it counts.
- [ ] There is one small scheme, set up and paid off with a prop.
- [ ] There is at least one hint of the secret, as a question or an image, and nobody says it aloud.

**Score and gates**
- [ ] Is every scene spotted? Does no family appear more than 3 times? Do the act-out outs rotate? Does the record play dry? (§9)
- [ ] Is the [POV ledger](pov-and-framing.md#83-the-pov-ledger-one-per-episode-filled-in-by-the-editor) clean? Does the episode pass the [guardrails pre-lock checklist](guardrails.md#8-pre-lock-checklist) and meet the fairness floor? Does the laugh density hold on the engagement map?

## 9. The score: one theme, many tones

### 9.1 Was it planned?

**Not beyond the main title and the stings.** Here is what existed as of 2026-09-25:
- **The main title.** "The Knee" is locked as V1 (chip-chamber jazz), with V2–V4 as re-orchestrations ([audio/theme](../../audio/theme/)). The [intro cue sheet](../intro/cue-sheet.md) covers only the 30 s intro.
- **The reel temp beds.** [`audio/reel/reelbed.py`](../../audio/reel/README.md) builds all twelve story reels from one bed: felt piano and a chip pad in F minor. That's where the "same track the whole time" risk comes from, and the reel README says the beds aren't score.
- **The scripts' music notes.** Each script has about six `MUSIC:` notes, mostly the same act-out sting. The one-offs are a dry piano F, the chip waltz, the séance organ, the sad violin, the keynote-reel piano and the chord with no third.
- **A cost line.** "Score cue library" appears as one line (5–15 agent-h) in [production-estimates §4.1](../format/production-estimates.md), with no cue list behind it.
- **New today:** an OST engine is being built in [`audio/ost/`](../../audio/ost/). The engine modules are there, but `tracks/_template` and `tracks/_demo` are still empty.

So the pipeline is starting, but there was no track list and no tone palette anywhere. **This section is the writers' brief for the OST:** the tones the show needs, what each one is for, and how scripts call for them. The audio owner owns the tracks.

### 9.2 Principles

1. **One score, not a medley.** Every track is built from the knee motif's cells (F F F F G A♭ C F) and the open fifth, then re-orchestrated. That's the cue sheet's own principle ([§1](../intro/cue-sheet.md#1-concept), [§3](../intro/cue-sheet.md#3-the-knee-motif)).
   - F minor is home, and D♭ and C are colour keys.
   - The arrival of the third stays reserved for Ep12.
2. **Score the stakes, never the joke.** The music believes the drama. Punchlines get no comic scoring: the cue thins or holds, or a sound effect takes the beat. No comic stingers after punchlines, no mickey-mousing, no meme sounds.
3. **The record plays dry** ([flow-and-continuity §3](flow-and-continuity.md#3-sound-a-continuous-bed), which replaces the old "no music under real lines, quote cards or the candor card, nor within the protected bars").
   - Under real lines, posts, quote cards and the candor card the music **thins and ducks** rather than stopping: melody and hits drop out, a sustained pad or bass pedal holds. "Dry" means no comic scoring, no stinger or rimshot, and no melody on the line.
   - No sting or swell under V.O. The score thins to one instrument under it; the V.O. sits inside the bed, not in a hole.
   - A full stop for the record is a designed beat (the D6 drop-out), rare and with room tone under it.
4. **Thin under Mas, swell under the world.** His calm means fewer instruments.
5. **Play each sequence as one continuous performance** ([flow-and-continuity §3](flow-and-continuity.md#3-sound-a-continuous-bed)). Change tone on a phrase boundary: a crossfade on a downbeat, a ring-out, or a pre-lap of the next cue under the outgoing picture. Cues carry across cuts: that's the cheapest bridge in §4. A fragment of a second or two sounds like a mistake unless it's a designed sting on a story beat.
6. **Rotate** (guides; the aim is that no episode sounds like one track on repeat).
   - A family used more than about 3 times in an episode starts to wallpaper.
   - Vary the act-out outs: avoid the same out twice in a row, or twice in one episode.
7. **Fair and clean.**
   - **Musical parity:** each administration gets motifs of equal weight ([guardrails §2a](guardrails.md#2a-the-rules), rule 4).
   - **Nothing coded or borrowed:** no culturally coded motifs (X10; for THE WHALE, no gongs or pentatonic stings), no liturgical quotation, no wedding march (X2), and no heartbeat, breath or pulse figures (X3). The thriller pulse comes from the rack LEDs and clock ticks.
   - **Original only:** reference other scores for their function only, never as sound-alikes. Check AI-music terms first ([guardrails §5](guardrails.md#5-legal-hygiene), [cue sheet §5.4](../intro/cue-sheet.md#54-licensing-caveats)). The route follows the distribution decision ([overview §9](overview.md#9-decisions-still-open)).
8. **Budget the score, never the room.** About 10–13 minutes of score in a 22-minute episode is a starting estimate. Where the score rests, room tone and ambience keep running under every shot: the drop-out and a few designed quiet beats are the only real silences ([flow-and-continuity §3](flow-and-continuity.md#3-sound-a-continuous-bed)).

### 9.6 How scripts call for music

```
MUSIC: LEVERAGE (low) · in: under the check · out: drops on the turn ("It's long-term.")
MUSIC: DARK ROOM (solo piano) · in: pre-lap 1 bar · out: tail into room tone
MUSIC: KEYNOTE REEL · 1 bar · out: stop mid-phrase on the downbeat
MUSIC: PROCEDURE continues · thin under the record (pad only, ducked) · no melody on the line
```

Every scene is spotted in the script. The `in` and `out` words come from this list:
- **in:** `cut`, `pre-lap`, `under`, `crossfade`
- **out:** `cut`, `tail`, `ring-out`, `crossfade`, `pre-lap`, `stop mid-phrase`, `drop-out`
- **under words:** `duck`, `thin` (the cue keeps playing; see §9.2 rule 3)

Spot by **sequence**, not by shot: one continuous cue per sequence, rendered to its length or edited on phrase boundaries ([flow-and-continuity §3](flow-and-continuity.md#3-sound-a-continuous-bed)). A `stop mid-phrase` or `drop-out` is punctuation, a few per act at most as a guide, each on a story beat with room tone under it.

### 9.7 Production (for the audio owner)

- **The library:** a stem library of 4-bar phrases for each family, at 2–3 intensities, each with a clean ending, so a cue can be rendered to a sequence's length or edited on phrase boundaries with crossfades and ring-outs ([flow-and-continuity §3](flow-and-continuity.md#3-sound-a-continuous-bed)). It uses the ten stem families the engine already routes: piano, strings, winds, brass, bass, drums, perc, chip, synth and fx.
- **The grid:** 96 BPM is the house tempo, because the whole library then fits together. Change the energy with half-time, double-time, 3/4, 6/8, and swing versus straight rather than tempo changes. The grid organises the music; it doesn't set where picture cuts land (dialogue cuts on story).
- **Season size:** about 45–60 cues (15 families with their variants, plus 1–3 room colours per episode), which makes an album of about 20 tracks.
- **The reel beds:** rebuild the story-reel temp beds from the library, so each reel previews its own palette.
- **Cost:** likely at or above the top of production-estimates §4.1's 5–15 agent-h line. The audio owner prices it.
- **Timing:** write a score doc in `audio/ost/` (the families, the spotting notation and a motif sheet) before Ep1's animatic.
