# Dialogue craft: conversations that feel natural and still move (research, 2026-09-26)

> **Showrunner, 2026-09-26, on the Act Four v4 animatic:** "it is getting cloesr. however, a lot of the dialoge is unnatural and the cuts are still quite fast. a few time dialogue seems to randomly blurt out or cut off. it seems since v2 you didn't allow any conversation to play out for more than a few seconds which makes it hard to follow. i said to cut down empty time, but not to cut every dialogue into only a few words per character"

**What this is.** A script consultant's craft reference on how nine prestige thriller and satire titles write and stage conversations, and fifteen rules for MR. MAS drawn from them. It supports [flow-and-continuity §4](../../bible/flow-and-continuity.md#4-dialogue-rhythm) ("Conversations play out"). Like everything in the bible, it's guidance: the test is whether a scene is fluid, clear and entertaining. The [guardrails](../../bible/guardrails.md), parody names ([naming](../../bible/naming.md)), verbatim real quotes with their tags, `[INVENTED]` marks, the POV contract and the no-spoiler rule stay firm.

**How far to trust it.**
- **Checked this session** (marked **(checked)**): the handful of production facts fetched from the Wikipedia pages listed under [Sources](#sources).
- **From memory** (marked **(from memory)**): scene descriptions, episode placements and interview claims. They're widely reported, but they weren't re-checked today; the web-search budget had run out.

## Short answer

- **The fast shows are fast because the talk has momentum, not because the lines are short.** Sorkin's lines are long and delivered quickly. Succession's scenes run minutes, with the camera hunting reactions. Veep's insults are complete, crafted sentences. The Big Short cuts jaggedly but lets its explanations run whole.
- **Conversations play out.** In all nine, a talk scene usually runs one to four minutes and has a shape: an ask, resistance, a turn. People speak in complete thoughts, and the main speaker regularly gets two or three sentences in a row.
- **Line lengths vary.** One-word answers exist everywhere, but they land because the lines around them are long. When everyone is terse, nobody's terseness means anything.
- **Interruptions and overlaps are motivated and mostly rare.** Succession and Veep use the most, and mainly in crowded rooms; even there the key line lands clean. Better Call Saul, Mr. Robot, Barry and Fleabag use silence and awkward pauses far more than cut-offs.
- **Exposition hides inside a fight.** A deposition, a cross-examination, a board vote, a sceptical team, a family dinner: the fact arrives as a weapon, a defence or an accusation.
- **Coverage holds.** Two-shots, over-the-shoulders, walk-and-talks and locked wides carry whole exchanges. Cuts come on turns (a shift in power, new information, a reaction that matters), and lines cross cuts.
- **What MR. MAS is doing differs on every one of these points**, for reasons that can be traced to specific rules in the bible ([§6](#6-where-the-bible-pushes-the-other-way)).

## 0. Where MR. MAS is now

**The lead's measurements of Act Four v4 (4:11):**
- 45 lines, 176 words; median 4 words per line.
- 21 lines of three words or fewer (47%); 5 lines cut off with a dash.
- 21 exchanges, averaging 2 lines; the longest conversation runs 10 s.
- Dialogue fills under 26% of the runtime, about 42 words a minute overall.
- The Ep1 Acts 1–3, Ep2 and Ep3 teleplays show the same pattern: a median of 3–4 words per line, about half the lines three words or fewer.

**Against the reference shows (all estimates, from memory):**

| | Act Four v4 | Typical in the nine titles |
|---|---|---|
| A talk scene | 2 lines, about 5 s; the longest 10 s | 1–4 min, often 10–30 lines; set pieces much longer |
| Median words per line | 4 | About 6–12, depending on the show and the character |
| Lines of three words or fewer | 47% | Perhaps 15–25%, clustered at turns and punchlines |
| Longest single speech | about 10 words | 30–100+ words, at least once in most episodes |
| Cut-offs with a dash | 5 in 4 min | A few per episode, in heated scenes |
| Speaking rate while talking | brisk | About 140–180 words a minute; Sorkin faster |

**Why it happened.**
- The post-v2 tightening pass cut talk instead of dead time.
- Guide line 6 and R8 in [tone-and-dialogue](../../bible/tone-and-dialogue.md) ("Collide, don't volley"), with the §7 checklist asking for an overlap or cut-off in every scene, pushed every exchange toward a quip.
- The per-character budgets in tone-and-dialogue §6 (Mas 8 words or fewer, Gerg 6 or fewer, Alyi one sentence at a time) each make sense for one character. Applied together, they leave no one in a scene who talks in paragraphs.
- The temp voices make it worse. A Kokoro stock voice on a two- or three-word line has no breath and no lead-in, so the line starts at full attack and reads as a blurt. A dash cut-off in a synthetic take has no performed interruption (no rising pitch, no catch), so it sounds like an edit error rather than a character being cut off.

## 1. What the nine share

1. **A scene is a negotiation that plays out.** Someone wants something, the other side resists, deflects or bargains, and something turns. The turn gets the room it needs: a real argument has several exchanges, not one.
2. **Complete thoughts.** Even the terse characters (Mike in Better Call Saul, Barry, Elliot aloud) speak in whole sentences. Their brevity is a choice the scene makes visible against others' length.
3. **Momentum, not brevity.** Pace comes from quick pickups, from each line demanding an answer, from entering late and leaving on the turn. It doesn't come from shaving words.
4. **Indirect answers.** People answer the question before, the subtext, or with a question. The answer is still followable, and it tells you what the speaker wants.
5. **Specific detail.** Numbers, names, objects in the room, jargon used correctly. It sounds lived-in, and it smuggles exposition.
6. **People talk past each other.** Two agendas in one exchange; the tension and the comedy live in the gap.
7. **Interruption with a motive.** Rank, heat, panic, a phone. Crowded rooms get crosstalk; two-handers mostly don't.
8. **Coverage that holds.** The camera stays on a relationship while it changes, and cuts when something changes.

## 2. Show by show

Each entry: what to borrow, exchange and line lengths (estimates), overlaps, exposition, coverage, and one or two paraphrased illustrations.

### 2.1 Succession (HBO, 2018–23): board rooms and phone calls

- **What to borrow:** long, messy power conversations where everyone has an agenda, and phone calls played on the listener.
- **Lengths (est.):** talk scenes usually run 1–4 minutes. Lines swing from one-word jabs to 30–50-word riffs (Roman's insults, Logan's tirades, Tom's self-pitying spirals). Median perhaps 6–9 words.
- **Overlaps:** the most on this list after Veep, and concentrated in family group scenes, where siblings talk over each other to claim the room. Two-handers (Kendall and Logan, Tom and Greg) mostly play clean. The line that turns the scene is almost always delivered whole.
- **Exposition:** corporate mechanics (votes, deals, board seats, succession clauses) arrive as ammunition. Nobody explains a tender offer; someone uses it to threaten someone.
- **Coverage:** predominantly handheld, 35 mm, and styled on Dogme 95 and the mockumentary; the cinematographer described it as voyeuristic and "non-cinematic", with close coverage and frequent zooms that feel like eavesdropping **(checked)**. Several cameras run the whole scene, so the edit can cut to a reaction in the middle of a line without clipping the line **(from memory)**.
- **Illustration: the phone sequence in "Connor's Wedding" (S4E3).** The siblings learn of their father's death over a phone held up by someone on a plane. It was a 28-page scene in the script, filmed in a 30-minute long take with cameras hidden around the set so one was always running, and Tom's lines were performed live over the phone from London **(checked)**. What matters for us: the scene is long, halting and repetitive the way real shock is. People ask the same question twice, talk to the phone and to each other at once, and the camera stays on the listeners. Nothing is clipped for pace, and it's the most gripping scene in the show.
- **Illustration: the vote-of-no-confidence meeting (S1E6, "Which Side Are You On?").** Kendall's attempt to oust Logan plays as a long procedural board scene: positions are restated, allies are counted, someone wavers, and the tension is who flips **(from memory)**. The procedure is the fuse; the dialogue is people trying to stay on the right side of it.
- **Take for MR. MAS:** the board's pass (sc 27) and Mas's calls can be Succession-length. Stay on the listener while the voice comes through the speaker.

### 2.2 The Social Network (2010): the deposition and the Winklevoss scenes

- **What to borrow:** dense talk delivered fast, a formal frame (the deposition) that licenses long answers and sharp interruptions, and status comedy.
- **Lengths (est.):** scenes of 2–5 minutes. Lines are long: a median of perhaps 10–14 words, with speeches of 60 words or more. The opening breakup scene took 99 takes **(checked)**; it is widely reported as about eight or nine pages of script playing in about five minutes, and the whole script as far longer than its runtime would suggest at a page a minute, because the actors talk fast **(from memory)**.
- **Overlaps:** the opening scene runs two conversations at once: Mark on the final clubs, Erica on their relationship. Each hears only the parts that suit them. It's overlapping *topics* more than overlapping words. In the depositions the lines are clean, and interruptions come from the setting (a lawyer objecting, a question cutting off an evasion).
- **Exposition:** Sorkin built the structure around three versions of the story, which "is how I came up with the structure of the deposition room" **(checked)**. The deposition is exposition as combat: the lawyers state what happened as accusations, and Mark's answers, contemptuous and precise, tell us who he is while they tell us the facts.
- **Coverage:** locked, precise singles and over-the-shoulders; cuts are frequent, but the talk runs whole across them, so the pace is the dialogue's rather than the cutting's **(from memory)**. The Winklevoss twins were one actor, with a body double and a digitally grafted face or split screens **(checked)**, which is why their scenes often hold a clean two-shot.
- **Illustration: the twins in the Harvard president's office.** The twins make a careful, reasonable case; the president dismisses them in a long, bored, withering reply about what Harvard students do with their ideas **(from memory)**. Nobody is cut off. The comedy is the gap between their earnestness and his impatience, and it needs every word of both.
- **Illustration: the twins arguing among themselves about suing.** One brother wants to stay gentlemen, the other wants to fight. The argument is about honour and class, and the exposition (what was taken, what it's worth) rides inside it **(from memory)**.
- **Take for MR. MAS:** a formal frame (a board call, a charter reading, a hearing) lets someone talk at length and lets an interruption feel earned. Two conversations at once is how people talk past each other without dashes.

### 2.3 Mr. Robot (USA, 2015–19)

- **What to borrow:** the unreliable narrator's V.O. against clipped, evasive speech aloud; formal, patient villains; silence as pressure.
- **Lengths (est.):** dialogue scenes are slow and often run 2–6 minutes. Elliot's spoken lines are short and evasive (median perhaps 4–7 words), but the scene around him isn't: Krista, Tyrell, Whiterose and Price speak in full, often formal sentences, and Whiterose and Price get long speeches on power and time.
- **Overlaps:** rare. The show uses pauses, stares and uncomfortable silence instead of cut-offs.
- **Exposition:** the V.O. to "friend" (the viewer as accomplice), influenced by *Taxi Driver* **(checked)**, carries the hacking and the paranoia. The therapy sessions are exposition inside conflict: Krista pushes, Elliot withholds, and what he withholds is the information.
- **Coverage:** characters are placed at the very bottom of the frame, leaving huge headroom that suggests weight and isolation **(checked)**. Singles are short-sided and held. Esmail directed every episode from season 2 on **(checked)**; one season-3 episode was stitched from about 40 shots to look like a single take **(checked)**. A late-season episode plays as a near-theatrical chamber piece: a few people in a house, in long dialogue scenes **(from memory)**.
- **Illustration:** a Whiterose conversation plays as one long, courteous, menacing monologue with the other person barely speaking, and the menace is in the courtesy **(from memory)**.
- **Take for MR. MAS:** Mas can be the terse one, like Elliot aloud, with his V.O. and the Orb as his interior. That works only if the people across from him talk in full.

### 2.4 Silicon Valley (HBO, 2014–19): board and investor scenes

- **What to borrow:** tech-industry board comedy that stays specific, and flat-affect power.
- **Lengths (est.):** meeting scenes run 1–3 minutes, with lines of perhaps 7–10 words and rants of 40 or more (Erlich, Gavin, Russ).
- **Overlaps:** bickering (Gilfoyle and Dinesh) comes in complete, crafted insults traded in turn. The board scenes use awkward silence far more than crosstalk.
- **Exposition:** hidden in pitches, votes and people explaining tech to investors who don't get it. Richard loses the CEO job in season 2 because Raviga has taken three of Pied Piper's five board seats **(checked)**, and the vote count is the scene's weapon. The show's jargon was real: the fictional compression metric was built for the producers by a Stanford professor **(checked)**.
- **Coverage:** conventional masters, singles and two-shots, with comic holds on the silence after something terrible is said.
- **Illustration: Laurie Bream.** The investor who replaces Peter Gregory, "highly intelligent and socially inept" **(checked)**, delivers devastating decisions in long, flat, perfectly formed sentences. Her length and calm are the joke, and the power.
- **Illustration: the season-1 finale's whiteboard scene.** A crude hypothetical turns, over a long, escalating exchange of real technical detail, into the insight that saves the company **(from memory)**. The scene is long, and every line adds a step.
- **Take for MR. MAS:** Neleh and Mada are our Laurie Bream: precise, flat, complete. Board scenes can run as procedure with a fuse, and the comedy is in how calmly terrible things are said.

### 2.5 Veep (HBO, 2012–19)

- **What to borrow:** speed and density in crowded rooms; insults as complete, crafted sentences; walk-and-talks.
- **Lengths (est.):** scenes of 1–3 minutes, many as corridor walk-and-talks. Lines of perhaps 8–12 words, with insult arias of 20–40 words.
- **Overlaps:** the most on the list. Staff talk over each other in crowded rooms, but Selina's lines, and each insult, land whole. The overlap is the room's noise, not the scene's key line.
- **Exposition:** blame. Every crisis (a leak, a gaffe, a poll) makes everyone say what happened while trying to pin it on someone else.
- **Coverage:** single-camera **(checked)**, handheld and documentary-styled, panning between speakers **(from memory)**. The cast had several months of rehearsal to get comfortable improvising together, and the first four seasons had an all-British writing staff from *The Thick of It* **(checked)**. The improvisation fed the scripts; the shows are still tightly written **(from memory)**.
- **Illustration:** Selina and her staff striding a corridor, three problems discussed at once, each person finishing their own sentence while the next one starts, and the scene ending the moment they reach the door **(from memory)**. It's fast because they're moving and because the scene ends on arrival, not because the lines are short.
- **Take for MR. MAS:** when the room is crowded (the all-hands, the 2 AM boardroom), crosstalk is right. Keep it to the room and keep the key line clean.

### 2.6 Better Call Saul (AMC, 2015–22)

- **What to borrow:** patience; long lines that are character; interruptions saved for eruptions.
- **Lengths (est.):** scenes of 2–6 minutes are common. Lines of perhaps 8–12 words; Jimmy's patter runs long, and Chuck's speeches run 80 words and more. Mike's replies are short but complete, and his silences are loaded.
- **Overlaps:** rare. When one comes, it's an eruption the whole scene has built to.
- **Exposition:** "Chicanery" (S3E5) turns a hearing into cross-examination as a trap. Jimmy establishes Chuck's condition through questions, and then reveals that the phone battery Chuck has carried for nearly two hours was planted on him **(checked)**. The backstory is the weapon. In "Pimento" (S1E9), Jimmy confronts Chuck, and Chuck answers with a long, brutal, unbroken explanation of why Jimmy should never be a lawyer **(checked)**. The scene is quiet, and nobody cuts anybody off.
- **Coverage:** composed wides, locked frames and long holds; singles and over-the-shoulders cut on turns. Lines are allowed to breathe **(from memory)**.
- **Take for MR. MAS:** let a character who has been polite for a whole act finally say the long thing. It lands because nobody interrupts.

### 2.7 The Big Short (2015)

- **What to borrow:** long explanations delivered inside scepticism, fast cutting that doesn't clip the talk, and the fourth wall as a joke on exposition itself.
- **Lengths (est.):** scenes of 1–4 minutes; pitch speeches and explanations of 80 words or more; lines of perhaps 10–15 words.
- **Overlaps:** improvised crosstalk inside Mark Baum's team **(from memory)**, but the explanations are delivered in full.
- **Exposition:** celebrity cameos explain mortgage-backed securities, CDOs, credit default swaps and synthetic CDOs, which was McKay's idea **(checked)**. Inside the drama, exposition hides in conflict: a trader pitches the short to a team that assumes he's lying, and stacks Jenga blocks to show the tranches while they heckle **(from memory)**. At a Las Vegas dinner, Baum interrogates a CDO manager who proudly explains how bad it really is **(from memory)**.
- **Coverage:** handheld, with snap zooms and jagged, award-winning editing **(checked: the editing awards)**. The lesson is that fast cutting and long talk coexist: the picture jumps, the conversation doesn't.
- **Take for MR. MAS:** THE PLAN blueprints are our cameo explanations. In the rooms, facts go to someone who doubts them.

### 2.8 Barry (HBO, 2018–23)

- **What to borrow:** deadpan held in wide frames; a terse lead whose terseness shows against others' length; people talking past each other.
- **Lengths (est.):** scenes of 1–4 minutes. Barry's own lines are short and halting (perhaps 2–6 words), and everyone else talks: NoHo Hank in cheerful, over-formal paragraphs, Gene Cousineau in grandiose monologues, Fuches in manipulative wheedling.
- **Overlaps:** rare. Awkward pauses and wrong-footed replies do the work.
- **Exposition:** the acting class. Barry's "scene work" exposes his real past to people who take it as acting, so exposition and dramatic irony are one move **(from memory)**. The show calls itself a crime tragicomedy about trauma, morality and violence **(checked)**.
- **Coverage:** wide, held frames where the comedy happens inside the shot, and a willingness to leave the camera still while people talk **(from memory)**. Hader directed episodes throughout and every episode of season 4 **(checked)**.
- **Take for MR. MAS:** this is Mas's model. His brevity is funny and unsettling only because the people around him talk more: Neleh in citations, Tasya in welcomes, Mario in clauses.

### 2.9 Fleabag (BBC, 2016–19)

- **What to borrow:** a private channel to the audience inside long, naturalistic social scenes, polite surfaces with knives under them, and a confession that plays whole.
- **Lengths (est.):** scenes of 1–5 minutes. The asides are quick (a look, a few words), but the scenes around them are long. The series-2 opening dinner runs much of the first episode as one multi-character scene **(from memory)**.
- **Overlaps:** social and polite: people talk past each other (the godmother's compliments that are insults, Claire and Martin), rarely over each other.
- **Exposition:** the protagonist "frequently breaks the fourth wall, providing exposition, internal monologues, and running commentary" **(checked)**. In series 2, the priest begins to notice her breaking it **(checked)**, so the device becomes a character beat. The show began as a one-woman stage show **(checked)**, and it keeps a monologuist's trust in long speech.
- **Illustration: the confession (series 2, from memory: episode 4).** She speaks for a long time, in a single held shot, with only minimal prompts from the priest. What she has withheld all series arrives there, whole.
- **Take for MR. MAS:** the Orb noticing Mas is our version of the priest noticing the asides. Mas's V.O. can be quick, like the asides, if the scenes around it are allowed to be long.

## 3. Coverage in pixel art

Pixel production can't shoot a scene with three cameras, but it can hold.

- **Hold on the relationship.** A two-shot or an over-the-shoulder pair can carry a 30–60 s exchange, with a cut when the power moves. A 45-s conversation might use 3–6 drawings, not one per line.
- **A push instead of a cut.** A slow whole-pixel push or drift over a held drawing keeps a long exchange alive (the budgets in edit-plan §7 apply).
- **Cut on turns, not on speakers:** when new information lands, when a reaction matters more than the speaker, or when the geography needs re-establishing ([flow-and-continuity §2](../../bible/flow-and-continuity.md#2-cutting)).
- **Let lines cross cuts.** Hear the answer before we see who gives it (a J-cut), or land a line's second half on the reaction (an L-cut). v4.1 already does this with the memo line over the nameplate.
- **Walk-and-talk:** a wide drawing with a lateral pan or parallax as two figures cross it; the conversation ends when they reach the door.
- **Phone and call scenes:** stay on the listener, with the caller in the speaker or the call's caption, as Succession does. Cut to the caller only when their face changes the scene. A split is for when both reactions matter at once (the Mario split).
- **Posts and cards join the conversation.** Someone reads the post out, someone disputes what it means, and the talk carries on around it. The record walks into a scene that's already talking, instead of replacing the talk.
- **Budget honestly.** A held two-shot with mouths, blinks and small gestures is cheaper to draw than six singles. Longer conversations can cost less art, not more.

## 4. Fifteen rules for MR. MAS dialogue

These are guides, like everything in the bible. Break one when the scene plays better, and say why in a line.

1. **Build scenes on conversations.** Someone wants something from someone, the other side resists, deflects or bargains, and it turns. Let it run as long as the turn needs: often 20–90 s and 6–16 lines. A conversation under 10 s is usually a button; check there's a scene around it.

2. **Speak in complete thoughts.** Each speaker gets at least one full sentence per exchange, and the scene's main speaker gets at least one line of two or three sentences. "No." answering a real question is complete. A fragment that only makes sense with the next line is the blurt the showrunner is hearing.

3. **Vary the lengths so the short lines hit.** As a guide, invented dialogue in a talk scene has a median of about 7–12 words and runs from one word to 30–40. A one-word answer lands because the question before it was long. If everyone is terse, nobody's terseness means anything: Mas's clipped lowercase reads as character only when the people around him talk in paragraphs.

4. **Write speech, not prose.**
   - Use contractions: "we're", "it's", "didn't", "I'd".
   - Start sentences the way people do: "Look,", "So,", "Okay, but", "I mean".
   - Let pressure show as a restart or self-correction ("It's not the vote. It's that nobody told the staff."), sparingly and in character.
   - Use pronouns for what everyone in the room already knows ("him", "that call", "it").
   - Avoid the written register in the room. "This board controls the company", said to board members, is a label. "We can fire him. That's the whole point of us." is a person. (The blueprint's charter reading is exempt: the device licenses the register.)

5. **Answer sideways, but answer.** People answer the question before, the subtext, or with a question. The indirect answer is still a full, followable reply that shows what the speaker wants. "When?" / "When it's ready." is a stall. "When?" / "Neleh, you've read the charter more times than anyone alive. Show me where it says we owe them a date." is a person dodging, and it tells a newcomer who reads the charter.

6. **Be specific.** Numbers, times, names, objects in the room, the footnote, the hotel Wi-Fi, the sandwich nobody ate. Specific detail sounds lived-in and carries exposition for free. Keep invented specifics on invented ground: texture ("I've read it four times tonight.") is free, but a detail that asserts a fact about a real person or company needs a tag or stays out ([guardrails](../../bible/guardrails.md)).

7. **Let people talk past each other.** Put two agendas in one exchange (Neleh on procedure and Alyi on meaning; Mario on risk and Adelina on the money; Tasya on welcome and Mas on terms). Each follows their own thread, and the scene's comedy and tension live in the gap. The lines are complete; it's the connection that misfires. This is the "collide" that R8 wanted, without the dashes.

8. **Hide exposition inside a fight.** A fact the newcomer needs arrives as a weapon, an accusation, a defence or a complaint, never as a statement to someone who already knows it. Tell it once, at the moment it hurts ([flow-and-continuity §5a](../../bible/flow-and-continuity.md#5a-clear-to-a-newcomer-the-real-coherence-test)). The record (posts, the letter, the blog post) becomes something people in the room react to and argue over.

9. **No blurts.** Before a line lands, the audience knows who is speaking, to whom, and what prompted it: a question, a look, an object, a sound, an event on screen. An off-screen voice gets a lead-in (a knock, a door, a name, a shot of the space it comes from). A line that opens a scene orients with its first words ("Rima, it's Neleh. Are you somewhere you can talk?").

10. **Interrupt with a motive, and after the thought lands.** Interruptions and overlaps are seasoning: a few per act, each with a reason the audience can feel (heat, rank, panic, a phone). Cut in once the interrupted speaker has said enough that we know where they were going, not mid-word by default. A calm scene has none; a crowded one (the all-hands, the 2 AM boardroom) can earn a burst of crosstalk while the key line stays clean. In the temp-voice animatic, prefer letting the line finish with the next speaker coming in over its last syllable: a synthetic voice doesn't perform being cut off, so a hard dash sounds like a render error.

11. **Keep it brisk by cutting air, not words.**
   - Enter late: skip the greetings and any first line that only warms up.
   - Leave on the turn.
   - Cut lines that repeat what the picture shows, and cut unmotivated holds.
   - Keep pickups tight: about 0.2–0.5 s on a quick reply, 0.6–1.2 s on a loaded one, a syllable of overlap when people are on each other.

   Speed comes from momentum: every line demands an answer. The working method is to write the scene long and complete, then cut whole lines and whole beats. Never shave every line down to its last three words.

12. **Cover the conversation, not the line.** Stage it in a held two-shot or an over-the-shoulder pair and cut on turns ([§3](#3-coverage-in-pixel-art)). Let lines cross cuts. Stay on the listener in a phone or call scene. A slow push is often better than a cut.

13. **Give each voice a rhythm, not a word cap.**
    - **Mas:** lowercase, gracious, withholding, the shortest speaker in any room, but complete. In private (with the Orb, with Gerg) he can run two sentences.
    - **Neleh:** precise and polite, in full sentences with sub-clauses and citations.
    - **Alyi:** slow and impersonal, a sentence like the end of a sermon, and now and then a second one.
    - **Gerg:** talks while typing, cheerful and technical, in whole sentences about the build.
    - **Tasya:** warm, unhurried, every welcome a lease clause.
    - **Mario:** adds clauses until someone stops him, which is the motive when Adelina cuts in.
    - **Mada:** silence and "Good question." His silence works because the others talk.

14. **Real lines sit inside a conversation.** Real quotes stay verbatim with their tags and are never clipped to fit a rhythm. A real line often arrives as a fragment (a post, a trimmed quote), so give it a setup the audience hears: an invented question it answers, someone reading it aloud, a reaction after, each marked `[INVENTED]`. A real line that lands cold, with no one asking and no one reacting, is a blurt too. Keep invented talk off the real event's motives ([tone-and-dialogue §5](../../bible/tone-and-dialogue.md#5-delivering-real-events-inside-the-drama)).

15. **Test by ear, then count to find spots.**
    - **Radio test:** listen with eyes closed. Can you tell who's speaking to whom, and what they want?
    - **Read it aloud at speed.** If it sounds like a trailer, it's clipped. If it sounds like a memo, it's written.
    - **Counts that point at scenes to look at, not gates:** the median words per line, the share of lines three words or fewer (above about a quarter, look again), the longest conversation (a talk-driven act usually has at least one of 30 s or more), and the number of dashes (one or two an act).
    - **For the temp voices:** record whole speeches in one take so the prosody carries across sentences, never time-compress a take to fit a picture, and cut the picture to the take.

AFTER (about 35–40 s, one two-shot with the reflection, then Neleh's close-up; all new words [INVENTED]):
```
The phones buzz and step: STAFF, STAFF, INVESTORS, STAFF.

       I've read it four times tonight.

       and I'd like to be able to tell them something.
ALYI (reflection, not turning): Step four will reveal itself.

ALYI (reflection; a loaded half-second later): That is the company telling us.
```
- Neleh talks in full, polite, precise sentences; Alyi answers the meaning, not the question. They talk past each other without a dash.
- The Rima callback reminds a newcomer who the interim CEO is without a plate. The phones stay the fuse.
- "That is the company telling us." lands harder with a real conversation in front of it.

**C. The Mario split, sc 27 (S4.08).** v4 cuts him off after five words.

MARIO: I've written up some thoughts—
ADELINA (overlapping): In plain English: no.

AFTER (a few seconds longer; new words [INVENTED]):

MARIO: Neleh, hi. Before you say anything, I've written up some thoughts.

ADELINA (taking the phone; brisk, warm): In plain English: no.

## 6. Where the bible pushes the other way

Flagged for the owner of [tone-and-dialogue](../../bible/tone-and-dialogue.md) and [pov-and-framing](../../bible/pov-and-framing.md). Nothing here was edited.

| Where | Now | Suggested direction |
|---|---|---|
| tone-and-dialogue, guide line 6 and R8 "Collide, don't volley" | Overlaps, cut-offs, wrong-source answers | Keep the anti-volley aim, and get it from people talking past each other and from indirect answers. Overlaps and cut-offs become seasoning with a motive (rules 7 and 10) |
| tone-and-dialogue §7, "Lines" | "Is there at least one overlap, a cut-off, or an answer from the wrong person or the TV?" | "Does the conversation play out, in complete thoughts? Do people collide when the scene is heated?" |
| tone-and-dialogue §6, Gerg's budget | "6 words or fewer" | Whole sentences about the build, cheerful and literal |
| tone-and-dialogue §6, Alyi's budget | "one sentence at a time" | Keep the sermon cadence; allow a second sentence |
| tone-and-dialogue §6 and pov-and-framing guide line 2, Mas's budget | 8 words or fewer, 20% of a scene's words or less | The 20% share is consistent with long conversations (it implies others talk more) and can stay. The 8-word cap works as his rhythm, but in private scenes he can run two short sentences |
| tone-and-dialogue §7, "Could the first two lines go?" and R5 "Enter late" | Cut the top of scenes | Cut greetings and warm-ups, not the setup of the ask; a line with no setup is a blurt |

## 7. Diagnostics

Measures to find scenes worth watching again, never gates:
- **Words per line:** median and distribution, per scene and per character. A scene where every speaker's median is under five words is worth a look.
- **Short lines:** the share of lines at three words or fewer. Above about a quarter, look again.
- **Conversation length:** the longest uninterrupted exchange (seconds and lines) per sequence.
- **Cut-offs and overlaps:** the count of dashes and `(overlapping)` per act, and whether each has a motive in the picture.
- **Blurt check:** for every line, can you name its trigger and its addressee from the picture and sound just before it?
- **Dialogue share:** the share of the runtime under speech. A set-piece act like Act Four won't be talk-driven throughout, but its talk scenes should be.
- **By ear:** the radio test and a read-aloud at speed. An agent can't do the listening part; it needs a human ear on the animatic.
